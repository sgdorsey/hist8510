1. What is one row?One row corresponds to one survey entry from the 1936 WPA records. This lets me count how many churches were included in the 1936 survey and all the information gathered about them as of that specific year. In the city database, each row is one city, which allows me to see how many mill-related churches were in each city and compare them to the locations of the largest mills and mill-dominated towns later. The pastor table includes the pastor’s name, years served, and a unique ID for each pastor in the records. This will allow me to easily keep up with pastors as they moved in and out of different mill villages while also allowing me to verify the records with denominational records of appointments.2. Where is your many-to-many, and why can a flat sheet not hold it?My many-to-many is the church_pastor table. It can’t be confined to a flat sheet because one pastor could have served at many churches over time or at once if part of a charge or circuit (where multiple churches were served by one minister), and one church could have had many ministers over time. Without a junction table, I would have had to either list each pastor multiple times with their years served and where they served.3. Where do your sources resist the categories you just gave them?Many of my churches started as union churches or changed locations since they were organized. To create a database, I chose to use the address that was listed on the survey form. However, some churches began with mill assistance in the form of a union church until they raised enough funds to build their own worship place without the mill’s help. In cases like this, I would need to figure out a way to denote a church that may not have been using mill-owned property at the time of the 1936 survey but had done so in the past, which I have not figured out how to address yet.
